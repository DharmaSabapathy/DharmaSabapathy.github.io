FRC 2468 — competition robot

Four seasons with Team Appreciate, a 150-student program in Austin. On the 2026 robot I ran integration for the whole machine and designed two of its subsystems from scratch. Everything here had to survive a competition weekend without anyone being allowed to fix it between matches.

On this page

  1. Full-robot integration
  2. Transfer subsystem
  3. Expanding hopper

Full-robot integration

On a student team the usual way a robot fails is not that a subsystem is bad. It is that six good subsystems, drawn in parallel against slightly different assumptions, cannot occupy the same volume. The intake wants the space the hopper claimed; the shooter needs a mounting face the drivetrain never provided. You find out in week five, and then you have no weeks left.

Master sketching

So the first thing built was not a part. I used a master sketch to define all subsystem interfaces, fix every space claim, and lay out the path a game piece travels through the robot — intake to transfer to hopper to shooter — as geometry that every subsystem then referenced. Anyone detailing a mechanism was working against a shared, controlled definition rather than their own idea of where the boundary sat.

Bringing it together

I integrated all six subsystems — drivetrain, shooter, intake, transfer, hopper and electrical — and worked through the conflicts that surfaced anyway. A master sketch reduces integration problems; it does not delete them. The remaining ones are the interesting ones, because by then the answer is always a trade between two subsystems that both have a reason to be where they are.

The V2 robot competed at the FIRST Championship.

Transfer subsystem

The transfer carries game pieces from the intake into the rest of the robot. I designed it from scratch and then rebuilt it: V1 proved the path, V2 made it something nobody had to touch between matches.

V1: prove the path

The first version ran live axles driving O-rings on 3-D printed pulleys. That is a fast way to find out whether the geometry moves a game piece at all — printed pulleys can be redrawn overnight, and O-rings are forgiving about alignment. It worked, and it told me what the second version needed to fix.

V2: dead axles and a timing belt

V2 moved to a timing belt and pulleys, dead-axled on custom stub rollers. Going dead-axle takes the rotating load off a through-shaft and puts it into bearings at each roller, and a timing belt removes the slip that O-rings tolerate by design. The result is a transfer whose speed is actually the speed you commanded.

Sharing a shaft with the intake

The last roller of V2 is integrated onto the intake pivot shaft as a dead axle. The shaft was already there, already located and already supported — so putting the roller on it removed a bearing block, a mount and a space claim from a part of the robot that had none of the three to spare.

Designed to be left alone

The whole of V2 was fastened with Nordlock washers and heavy-duty hardware so it would be maintenance-free. In a competition weekend the thing you cannot afford is a subsystem that needs re-torquing between matches — the pit time is not there, and a fastener that backs out during a match takes the robot with it.

Expanding hopper

A hopper that expands horizontally to hold game pieces and collapses back inside the frame. Two versions — and the second one is better because it does less.

Why it expands

Capacity and frame perimeter pull in opposite directions: the robot has to start inside a legal footprint but wants to hold more than that footprint allows once it is playing. A horizontally expanding hopper resolves that by being small when it is measured and large when it is working.

V1: active geometry

The first version used an active geometry slot to deploy and stow the hopper off the intake pivot. The slot did the work of a linkage — the pivot’s motion was translated into the hopper’s motion by the profile it followed.

V2: take the mechanism out

V2 dropped the driven slot and simply deploys when the intake deploys. It is less clever and considerably more reliable: fewer moving interfaces, fewer things to bind, fewer ways for the deployment to half-happen. That is usually the right trade on a competition robot, where the mechanism has to work unattended in front of a crowd rather than demonstrate ingenuity.

Making it survive the load

V2 carries eight sets of bearing stackups, offset to resist torque. A panel that swings out horizontally is a long lever arm on its pivots, and the load is not pure rotation — offsetting the stackups gives the assembly something to react the twist against instead of letting it work its way into the bearings.
